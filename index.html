Economist · MIT Center for Real Estate

Rongjie Zhang

Research Fellow
MIT Center for Real Estate
Massachusetts Institute of Technology

I am an economist interested in housing, cities, and the decisions people make within them. My work draws mainly on microdata from China to ask what happens when policy changes the rules of a market: who adjusts, how, and who ultimately bears the cost.

I have worked on housing purchase restrictions, land sales, migration, and the ways housing wealth shapes family choices. More recently, I have also been studying the labor-market consequences of heat protection and how cities and households respond to environmental risks. The questions vary, but I tend to start with a specific policy or institutional detail and see where the evidence leads.

Before coming to MIT, I was a Research Fellow at the National University of Singapore. I received my Ph.D. and bachelor’s degree from Tsinghua University, along with a second bachelor’s degree in economics from Tsinghua’s School of Economics and Management.

Selected research

01

Unused housing in urban China and its carbon emission impact

Hefan Zheng, Rongjie Zhang, Xinru Yin, and Jing Wu (co-corresponding author)

Nature Communications · 16, 1985 (2025)

We estimate that 17.4% of housing built in 2001–2020 remained unused by early 2021, producing 55.81 million tonnes of CO₂ annually.

02

Corruption stereotype and the unintended consequences of an anti-corruption campaign: evidence from the real estate sector in China

Hanming Fang and Rongjie Zhang (co-corresponding author)

Journal of Public Economics · 249, 105474 (2025)

After China’s anti-corruption campaign, officials became more reluctant to sell residential land to private developers, helping explain the relative rise of state-owned developers.

03

Migration Restrictions and the Migrant-Native Wage Gap: The Roles of Wage Setting and Sorting

Naijia Guo, Li Zhang, Ben Zou, and Rongjie Zhang

Revise & resubmit, American Economic Review

Studies how migration restrictions contribute to the migrant–native wage gap through firms’ wage-setting decisions and workers’ sorting across employers.

Working paper ↗
04

When Protection Hurts: Labor Market Consequences of Mandatory Heat Allowances in China

Sumit Agarwal, Kam C. Chan, Nianhang Xu, Ruoming Yang, and Rongjie Zhang

Revise & resubmit, Journal of Environmental Economics and Management

Using matched employer–employee records, we find that a mandatory heat allowance was partly offset by lower wages and greater dismissal risk, particularly at private firms.
